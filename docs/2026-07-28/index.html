Nuclear trivia—reveal

The answer: Braidwood is the nuclear power plant that most recently started up in Illinois. The plant’s first and second units entered commercial operation in July and October 1988, respectively. And the reverse: The nuclear power plant to most recently shut down in Illinois was Zion, where both units were permanently shuttered in January 1998. Zion is, in fact, the only commercial nuclear plant to be entirely shuttered in Illinois’s history. While Dresden-1 was closed in October 1978, the plant lives on with the continued operation of Dresden-2 and -3. Units 1 and 2 at Zion opened in 1973 and 1974, respectively. At the time of the plant’s closure, the Nuclear Regulatory Commission licenses for each unit were not set to expire until 2013. The reason for this premature closure is captured in the May 1997 issue of Nuclear News, where it was reported that the plant’s steam generators were in need of a replacement, which was set to cost utility Commonwealth Edison $400 million. To avoid this sizeable expense, the utility opted instead to let the station close. In the February 1998 issue of NN, a utility spokesperson explained the rationale: A thorough analysis of the projected costs to produce power at [Zion] and the expected price of electricity in a deregulated market led us to [conclude that Zion] will not be able to produce competitively priced power in a deregulated marketplace over the remaining useful life of the plant. The site was released by the NRC for unrestricted use in 2023. Back to the operational fleet: While Zion is the only NPP in Illinois to be fully shut down and dismantled, every other plant in the state at one time or another has had an uncertain future. A 2021 article on Nuclear Newswire chronicles that uncertainty, from the near closure of Clinton and Quad Cities in 2016 to the now-scuttled plans for the early retirement of Byron and Dresden. And the future: With its nuclear moratorium lifted earlier this year and communities across the state signaling interest, the future of nuclear in Illinois is looking brighter. An NN feature from February of this year, “From Uncertainty to Vitality: The Future of Nuclear Energy in Illinois,” takes an even closer look at what the road ahead looks like for the state.
